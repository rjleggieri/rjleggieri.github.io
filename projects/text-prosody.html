Text–prosody alignment

I turned a question about the relationship between language and voice into an interpretable score, an end-to-end multimodal study, and reusable software for evaluating affect models.

2ADS representation variants
5 foldsparticipant-level evaluation

The question I investigated

A transcript describes what someone said. Their delivery adds another stream of information. I investigated whether comparing model-derived affect across the two modalities could produce an interpretable feature associated with PHQ-8 scores.

Rather than leaving the relationship to an opaque fusion layer, I designed the Alignment Discrepancy Score (ADS): the Euclidean distance between text-derived and audio-derived coordinates in a valence–arousal representation. Computing the comparison at utterance level made it possible to examine both individual segments and patterns across an interview.

What I built

I implemented the pipeline from interview files through model evaluation, including the data joins, modality-specific feature extraction, ADS construction, and analysis outputs.

  1. Prepare and align the data. Build session manifests, attach PHQ-8 labels, extract participant-only transcript turns, and cut utterance-level audio clips using transcript timestamps.
  2. Extract complementary audio features. Compute MFCC statistics, RMS energy, and zero-crossing rate with librosa; run pretrained wav2vec 2.0 affect classifiers in batches and map their outputs into the affect representation.
  3. Compare text representations. Generate text embeddings and emotion predictions with DistilRoBERTa, alongside sentiment predictions from a CardiffNLP RoBERTa model. Construct separate emotion-based and sentiment-based ADS variants.
  4. Build session-level features. Roll utterance scores into mean, standard deviation, maximum, proportion above the high-ADS threshold, and longest high-ADS streak. This preserves information about spread and persistence as well as average discrepancy.
  5. Model and evaluate. Use standardized features with logistic regression for PHQ-group classification and ridge regression for score prediction. Compare feature sets using participant-level folds, then examine ROC/precision–recall behavior, calibration, operating points, and bootstrap intervals.
  6. Make processing inspectable. Record model-loading and inference failures, short or missing audio, alignment coverage, and metadata so that exclusions and feature availability can be traced through the pipeline.

What the study found

Sentiment-based alignment was the stronger formulation

The representation choice mattered. In the reported five-fold comparison, sentiment-ADS reached a mean AUC of 0.619, compared with 0.501 for emotion-ADS. The combined ADS feature set reached 0.578. That result gave the study a specific direction: the sentiment-based comparison carried more useful signal in this experiment.

Precision–recall analysis showed signal above the prevalence baseline

The saved out-of-fold operating-point analysis produced AUPRC 0.410 against a PHQ-elevated prevalence of 0.302, with pooled AUC 0.609. The pooled AUC and the mean fold AUC above summarize different evaluations; they are labeled separately here.

I also examined the operating-point trade-off. The balanced threshold produced approximately 74% sensitivity and 51% specificity. A sensitivity-oriented threshold reached approximately 81% sensitivity and 32% specificity. Reporting both sides of that trade-off made the model behavior more interpretable than a single headline accuracy.

The distribution analysis added another view of the result

Session-level medians were similar across PHQ groups, while the PHQ-elevated group showed a tighter score distribution with fewer high-ADS outliers. Looking at spread and tails, together with high-ADS proportions and streak features, made cross-modal variability a concrete subject of analysis.

In the saved ROC comparison, text-only prediction was stronger than sentiment-ADS, and sentiment-ADS exceeded the audio-only baseline. ADS contributed an explicit, inspectable comparison between the modalities, alongside the predictive information in each individual stream.

Research → engineering follow-through

Turning the research into reusable evaluation software

The study exposed a practical question: how should a comparison between independently trained affect models be evaluated? I developed text-prosody-evidence to make the grouping, calibration, reliability, and provenance behind that evaluation explicit and testable.

The Python framework implements contracts for paired references, connected-component grouping across related records, nested grouped evaluation, calibration sensitivities, reliability interfaces, paired bootstrap comparisons, and hash-addressed evidence manifests. Tests cover fold isolation, calibration behavior, invalid-interval handling, and artifact integrity.

An executed public text-component benchmark

I implemented a reproducible benchmark using 9,061 EmoBank sentences from 136 source documents, with five outer document-grouped folds, four inner folds for model selection, and 2,000 paired document-bootstrap replicates. The published test partition is excluded.

The paired RMSE difference was −0.0367, with a 95% interval of [−0.0444, −0.0301]. This is an observed improvement in the grouped text-component benchmark. Arousal error did not show the same resolved advantage.

This follow-through connects the research to usable software: a repeatable benchmark, explicit comparison rules, tests, and inspectable aggregate outputs.

Study context

The DAIC-WOZ results come from exploratory participant-level cross-validation, with thresholds selected within the analysis. The EmoBank benchmark tests text affect estimation. Validating the cross-modal measure would additionally require paired text/audio reference judgments and independent evaluation.
